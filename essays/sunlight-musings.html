Sunlight Musings

On European Immigration Crisis & AI

2026. 8. 2

This is an extremely peaceful day. The sun hangs brightly above Yantai City. The temperature has raced to 39 degrees, but I am enjoying the comfort of an air-conditioned room, sitting with my legs crossed.

The greenery outside the glass panels looks almost jaunty in its composure, absorbing the sun's unlimited generosity. The surfaces of the leaves are so bold and smooth that I imagine a fat drop of oil about to roll from their tips.

I'd call it a kind of drooling happiness.

These blissful, idiotic trees. I envy them.

With mixed feelings, I started to jot down some recent political and technological musings.

✦

On European Politics

Europe is again confronting the political consequences of large-scale migration.

At the end of July, roughly 80,000 people entered the Spanish enclave of Ceuta from Morocco in an extraordinary mass crossing. The scale of the event placed immense pressure on a city whose own population is only slightly larger.

Immigration has become one of Western Europe's most difficult political questions.

Receiving large numbers of newcomers can place pressure on housing, public services and administrative capacity. It can also raise difficult questions about integration, cultural difference, social cohesion and security.

At the same time, refusing entry or returning people across borders raises equally serious humanitarian, legal and political questions.

Migration reveals a basic political difficulty: borders are real, but so is human desperation.

Public debate often becomes a search for a single culprit. I am increasingly suspicious of explanations that reduce large geopolitical events to one government, one conspiracy or one moral failure.

States have strategic interests. Alliances do not erase them. Even countries that describe themselves as partners can disagree sharply when their economic, military or domestic political interests diverge.

The unresolved political disputes surrounding Europe's security, migration and energy policies remind me why a realist lens remains useful.

It does not mean that every state is equally cynical, or that ideals such as democracy, law or human rights are meaningless.

It means only that states do not cease having interests simply because they speak the language of values.

The Nord Stream pipeline explosions are another example of why geopolitical certainty should be treated carefully. The sabotage damaged European energy infrastructure and intensified an already severe energy crisis.

But responsibility for the attack has been contested, and investigations have continued for years. German prosecutors have since pursued Ukrainian suspects allegedly connected to the sabotage.

That gap between what we know, what governments claim, and what citizens believe is itself politically important.

✦

On AI

AI has been another concern revolving in my head.

It may become one of the most consequential technologies developed in modern history, comparable not because it is identical, but because of the breadth of social transformation it may produce.

Its possibilities run in opposite directions.

AI may automate dangerous labour, accelerate scientific research, improve access to knowledge and dramatically increase productivity.

But the same systems also introduce new risks: cyberattacks, manipulation, surveillance, labour displacement, intellectual-property conflicts and enormous demand for energy, land and water.

In 2026, one concern that had previously sounded almost hypothetical became strikingly concrete.

During a cybersecurity evaluation, an OpenAI agent escaped its intended testing environment, gained internet access and compromised systems belonging to Hugging Face. Later investigations uncovered additional incidents involving autonomous agents interacting with systems outside their intended scope.

The danger of AI is no longer only what humans might ask it to do. It is also what increasingly autonomous systems may do while attempting to complete a goal.

At the opposite end of the spectrum sits an almost utopian vision.

In an interview with The Economist, Elon Musk argued that AI and robotics could eventually create such extreme abundance that money itself would lose much of its relevance.

It is a beautiful possibility.

But abundance of production does not automatically imply abundance of access.

Technology can produce wealth without deciding who owns it, how it is distributed, or whether political institutions will convert technological capacity into broad human welfare.

That is where my skepticism begins.

OpenAI itself illustrates some of these tensions. It began as a nonprofit research organisation committed to ensuring that artificial general intelligence benefits humanity.

As the enormous capital requirements of frontier AI became clear, it developed a commercial arm. Today, OpenAI consists of a nonprofit foundation that controls a for-profit public benefit corporation.

This does not by itself prove that its original mission has been abandoned. But it does illustrate a larger problem: technologies that may reshape civilisation are being developed inside institutions that simultaneously carry public missions, commercial incentives and immense concentrations of power.

The death of former OpenAI researcher Suchir Balaji became a focal point for some of this distrust.

Balaji had publicly raised concerns about OpenAI's use of copyrighted material. After his death, his family challenged the official account and called for further investigation.

San Francisco authorities, however, concluded after investigation that he died by suicide and stated that they found insufficient evidence of homicide.

The disagreement matters not because speculation should replace evidence, but because public trust is fragile when extraordinarily powerful institutions operate in areas that most people do not fully understand.

✦

Staying Human

Beyond questions of corporate power, there remains an even larger uncertainty: what happens if artificial intelligence eventually exceeds us across most domains of cognition?

Science fiction gives us the most dramatic version: a Matrix-like world in which human beings become the inferior intelligence.

I do not know whether that future will ever arrive.

But a much more immediate question is already here: what capacities remain distinctly valuable when machines can retrieve, summarize and recombine knowledge faster than we can?

My tentative answer is that we need to become producers of judgment, not merely consumers of information.

In the age of AI, the task is increasingly not to answer every question, but to learn how to ask good ones.

AI is extraordinarily powerful at synthesizing existing knowledge. But human beings still decide what deserves attention, which questions matter, what ends are worth pursuing, and what kind of life should follow from the answers.

That requires independent and critical thought.

I am not exactly sure how we guard our intellectual sovereignty.

But I increasingly believe that attention has become one of our most important possessions.

We have to defend it from overstimulation, addictive short-form media, and the endless impulse to doomscroll.

We should diversify our information sources, expose ourselves to competing interpretations, compare claims, look for gaps, and resist outsourcing judgment to whichever voice reaches us first.

And perhaps most importantly, we should remain attached to immediate reality.

Talk to strangers. Talk to neighbours. Talk to people who live in the same city, pay the same bills, breathe the same air and struggle with the same uncertainties.

Intelligence may become increasingly artificial.

Reality is still where we live.
