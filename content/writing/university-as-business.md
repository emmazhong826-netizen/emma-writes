In the 1970s, America transitioned from a manufacture-based society to a consumption-based economy. Higher Education descends from a "public good" to a "private good", a consumer product for citizens who can afford the service. Scandalously, our university increasingly operated like business, subordinating the interests of students, pursuit of truth, to profit-making. In a text message, I wrote:

        "What disillusions me about the neo-liberalization of education is the fact that students are taken advantages of by the institutions and market force but we can do nothing against this, of the $500 textbooks, of the over $7000 compulsory meal plan as a first year student living in residence. The large-scale availability of student loan allows the institutions to drive up price of education without direct, immediate backlashes but it is ultimately the students who are forced to repay the loans after graduation.

        I’m also disappointed at the university’s value choice of prioritizing and investing in facilities, fashionable ideas/ideologies, and bureaucracies over truth (i.e., academic faculties and educational standards). As students, we are indeed dazzled by the grandeur, or the diversity of recreational and course choices in the beginning. But years into my study, I realized what is structurally missing in my education is how to “make choices” among these options, it’s cultivating a moral compass that grounds and directs my choices in the first place. I ended up getting distracted by all of the novelties and have difficulty knowing what I want. I feel indignant because I realize this gap is systematic (and out of a conscious betrayal of the university) because it profits off our confusion and youth!

        The truth is, even with so many alleged “support” (clubs, socials, mental health consultation, etc.), I feel we are a student body that grows ever more confused, depressed, and anxious because the fundamental cause of all problems is a lack of “why” in life that produce productivity. Students feel atomized, isolated, and have harder time making genuine friends because we don’t know how to form bonds other than from consumptive activities, all of which need to be informed by a liberal arts education.

        I don’t think we need newer dorms—the thicker walls and dead silence ironically separate us further and weaken our sense of belonging at student residence, neither do we need the skills and technicality from the ever-bloated bureaucracy on course and grades consultation, but we need a normative lesson: we need a genuine liberal education that teaches us independent thinking, meaning-making, and love that brings serenity to our hearts and light to this world in this life of finitude."

        Dec. 5th, 2024
