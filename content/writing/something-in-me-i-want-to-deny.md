I’m writing in English because there is something in me, some Chineseness in me that I so desperately want to deny.

Is that a good thing or a bad thing about Chinese culture?

Is it the parental care that treats you like a baby?

Or is it the mandatory imposition of the little empress syndrome?

Or could it be genuine care and love whose sweetness I avoid allowing myself to savor?

It is for sure isolation that I’m choosing for myself.

For the first time in my adult life, I have tended to my own feelings and trusted my own senses and judgements upon things.

Also for the first time, I have acted out.

I have consciously resisted a force—the force of my family that I know will simultaneously bring relaxation and fragmentation.

My family is a decent Chinese family.

Ma and Pa were socialized in the Reform and Opening era, and they think on utilitarian grounds.

They care for me in a way whose climax ends in the prelude of my own conception of life.

I yearn for a life of purpose.

They just want me safe and fed, living a stable life in secure employment.

This is NOT the life that I envisage for myself.

I don’t know what I want yet, but NOT THAT.

I’m in my early twenties.

I have so much energy to deposit, so much passion to pour into the world.

I want to make a difference.

I’m like any young idealist, and I have been mocked as such.

The difference is that I have a sense of self-respect now.

I imagine myself making some big noise in the far distance, and I yearn to exhaust my days for my striving.

My striving for now is to read, as much as I can, about psychoanalysis and cultural psychology as guides for my graduate program application.

I know one thing for sure.

I have acted against the tides.

The tide of culture and family.

I know it is not easy.

The uneasiness springs out of me in brimmed eyes, which I cannot help.

I’m important and I matter.

In the grander scheme of things, I don’t.

But I know I have inherent dignity as a human being, and I will not allow the reduction of my humanity by any external forces, be it family or society.

I also will not reduce myself.

I will live my day heartily, actively.

There will be boundaries that I set for myself now.

I know I’m really bad at it, but I’ll try every day, as hard as I can.

This has led to a question that bothers me a lot recently:

**What are the aspects of myself that I must not compromise?**

One unconstructive aspect of my relationships is my tendency to get too close, to blend, and subsequently, to get burned.

From people close to me, I witnessed and learned a form of individualism, one led by reason extended from one’s own being.

I used to reckon that thinking, feeling, and acting in accordance with others could only be benign altruism.

However, now I ponder—to what extent are these gestures involuntary, mere products of my socialization?

I do them simply because they feel right.

To initiate any action from an autonomous place requires the mediation of reason—or does it?

Reason must stem from a place of distance, stillness, and non-reaction.

I must be able to detach my heart from passion.

But at the same time, I question my framing and the feasibility of doing so.

Perhaps a more realistic picture of life is for us to consciously choose a passion, and thus the compulsions to which we commit ourselves.

I choose to live a more toned-down life, one with my heart half-withdrawn and half-shed instead of always on my sleeves, because I’m creating an environment in which my reason can work.

I don’t feel apologetic for myself anymore.

Impulsive and immersive emotions often lead to chaos, though also to self-forgetfulness, inspiration, and poetry.

I used to be young and naive.

There is a beauty in my naivety and unguarded kindness.

But there is another aspect of life—what people call the “truth of life.”

I’m starting to feel its bitterness.

And perhaps—

its liberation.
